01 · The problem

Damage that doesn't hurt, until it's late.

In Pakistani studies, about 29% of people with diabetes had diabetic retinopathy. It is treatable when caught early, but screening sits mostly in eye hospitals, far from where diabetes is treated.

In one clinic study, over 70% of long-standing diabetes patients had never had a retinal check.

Sources: IDF Diabetes Atlas · Mumtaz et al. 2018 · Jawa et al. 2016 · The News 2026

02 · The idea

One photo. One next step.

Proven AI screeners exist abroad, but they are tied to specific cameras, cloud servers or paid licences. Roshni adapts the same idea into a tool that runs in a web browser, on the device the clinic already has.

The photo never leaves the device, and after the first visit it works without internet.

REFER

Referable disease suspected.

Moderate or worse diabetic retinopathy may be present. The clinician confirms and refers to an eye specialist.

On screen: expert grade 3 (severe), good photo. Roshni's answer on this photo: Refer.

RETAKE

Too unclear to rule disease out.

A photo that can't be read is sent for a retake instead of an all-clear, and it never blocks a referral.

On screen: expert grade 0, poor photo. Roshni's answer on this photo: Retake.

RESCREEN

Not detected in this gradable photo.

Routine check-ups continue as the doctor advises. This is not a guarantee that the eye is healthy.

On screen: expert grade 0, good photo. Roshni's answer on this photo: Rescreen.

03 · How it works

Four steps, in your browser.

  1. UploadOne retinal photo from the clinic camera.
  2. Check image qualityA quality check flags photos too poor to read.
  3. AnalyseAn EfficientNet-B0 model scores referable disease, with a heat map of where it looked.
  4. ResultRefer, retake or rescreen, with a note in English and Urdu for the doctor to confirm.

05 · Where it fails

We show the misses too.

This photo is expert grade 2, referable, and Roshni gives it an all-clear. We show it on purpose.

  • Not yet tested on patients in Pakistan or on low-cost cameras.
  • At new hospitals the cut-off must be re-set on local photos.
  • Does not detect macular oedema. One photo per eye.
  • A research prototype: not a medical device and not a diagnosis.
